## Design Context

### Users

Five friends choosing one real group trip. They are casual players, not travel researchers; the product should get them from curiosity to an expressive preference signal in about three minutes, then help them explore the candidate set and have a good group conversation.

### Brand Personality

Playful, tactile, cinematic—and candid. It is a social game wearing the clothes of an editorial travel experience, not a booking funnel or a conventional survey. The interface explains what it knows in plain language instead of hiding behind mystical model language.

### Aesthetic Direction

Warm night-time adventure for the game loop, contrasted with bright, spacious editorial travel surfaces for discovery. Borrow the inspiration’s oversized friendly type, clear modular panels, soft depth, bold color blocking, card-stack motion, and map-like visual storytelling. Keep the existing amber/olive/terracotta system as the connective tissue; avoid generic travel-dashboard chrome. Destination photography is deliberately allowed as a soft cue during comparison, without names or geographic metadata.

### Design Principles

1. Explain the trick through a short, visual sequence, then get out of the way.
2. Make every choice feel like a game turn: large, balanced, tactile, and immediately responsive.
3. Use character art as social presence outside comparison cards.
4. Explain each post-game surface directly: the rhythm describes recurring qualities in chosen experiences; the atlas is the complete unranked candidate set.
5. Give a finished traveler their own private shortlist promptly, with a clear reminder not to share it; keep everyone else’s shortlist and the group tally sealed until the organizer opens the reveal.
6. Use motion as feedback and anticipation; never as the only way to understand state.
7. Make discovery accessible: visible selection state, keyboard-operable maps and galleries, 20px desktop copy minimum, and useful fallbacks when media or WebGL fails.
